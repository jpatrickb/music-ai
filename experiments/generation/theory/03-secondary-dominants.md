## Definition

A **secondary dominant** is a chord that functions as the dominant (V or V7) of a chord other than
the tonic. It temporarily "tonicizes" — treats as a local tonic — a non-tonic scale degree.

**Notation:** V/x or V7/x, where x is the chord being tonicized. **Example:** V7/vi = the dominant
seventh of the vi chord (in C major: E7 → A minor)

### The Non-Negotiable Requirement: The Leading Tone

> "The most important thing needed to create a secondary dominant is the inclusion of the LEADING
> TONE. Without the leading tone, it will NOT be a secondary dominant."

The leading tone of the _target_ chord — the raised 7th scale degree of that chord's key — must be
present. This is what gives the secondary dominant its directed, focused sound.

**Example in C major, targeting vi (A minor):**

- A minor's leading tone = G# (7th degree of A minor's harmonic scale)
- Therefore, V/vi must contain G#
- E7 (E–G#–B–D) contains G# → correctly functions as V7/vi ✓
- E minor (E–G–B) does NOT contain G# → not a secondary dominant ✗

## Three Methods for Creating Secondary Dominants

### Method 1: Bass Motion by a Fifth (Root Position)

Look for a chord whose bass note is a perfect fifth above the root of the target chord. This creates
the V–I bass motion.

**Process:**

1. Find the target chord (e.g., G major = V in C major)
2. The chord a fifth below G is D
3. A D-quality chord that has the leading tone of G (F#) → D major (or D7)
4. D major → G major = V/V → V in C major **"Low hanging fruit":** The ii chord moving to V is the
   most natural place to apply this method. The ii chord (e.g., D minor in C major) is already a
   fifth below V; raising its third (D minor → D major by adding F#) creates V/V.

```
Before: ii  —  V      (D minor → G major)
After:  V/V —  V      (D major → G major)
```

### Method 2: Fifth Relationship by Inversion

The chord is inverted, so the bass does not literally move by a fifth — but the _chord roots_ are
still a fifth apart. The secondary dominant function is identical.

**Process:** Same as Method 1, but the resulting secondary dominant chord appears in an inversion
rather than root position.

**Example:** D major triad in first inversion (F# in bass) going to G major — still V/V → V, even
though the bass moves by a third rather than a fifth.

### Method 3: Total Respelling (Creating from Scratch)

Through chromatic respelling — adding accidentals — create a chord with the correct notes to
function as a secondary dominant, even if no obvious fifth-bass relationship exists.

**Process:**

1. Identify the target chord
2. Build a major triad (or dominant seventh) on the pitch a perfect fifth below that chord's root
3. Notate with the necessary accidentals **Guiding principle:** "If it sounds good, it is good."
   Trust the ear after verifying the leading tone is present.

**Example:** V7/vi (E7) in C major requires adding a G# to what would otherwise be an E minor chord
— a total chromatic respelling of the iii chord into a major-quality chord.

## Voice Leading Cautions

1. **Avoid cross relations** — the most common problem when inserting secondary dominants. If you
   raise a pitch to create a leading tone, check that the same pitch class does not appear unraised
   in another voice in the same or adjacent chord.
2. **Smooth the surrounding harmonies** — secondary dominants can create awkward leaps in inner
   voices. You may need to adjust adjacent chords to maintain smooth voice leading.
3. **Melody check** — when arranging with a fixed soprano (e.g., a hymn congregation melody), verify
   that the secondary dominant's notes do not conflict with the given melody.

## Augmented Triads as Chromatic Color Chords

_(Distinct from secondary dominants — no dominant function)_

An **augmented triad** is a stack of two major thirds (e.g., C–E–G#). It is a chromatic color chord,
not a secondary dominant, because it contains no leading tone of a target chord.

**Function:** Local chromatic inflection — a "very temporary color" that likely functions as a
chromatic passing event. Does not change the tonic center.

### Two Methods of Use

**Method A — Chromatic Passing Tone in Melody:**

- Raise a melody note by a half step to create an augmented triad
- **Warning:** This changes the melody. Only appropriate in arrangements (choir, prelude); **never**
  when accompanying a congregation singing the melody. **Method B — Augment a Static Triad for
  Harmonic Motion:**
- When a chord is held for multiple beats without harmonic change, raise the fifth by a half step on
  one of the inner beats to create an augmented triad
- Does not change the melody, so it can be used safely with a congregation
- Effect: replaces a static, "bland" measure with expressive chromatic motion **Example in C
  major:** C–E–G (plain I) → C–E–G# (augmented I on beat 3) Roman numeral: I → I⁺ → I

## Diminished Secondary Dominants and the Implied Leading Tone

### The vii° Family

In any key, the leading-tone triad (vii°) is closely related to V7:

| Chord           | Pitches (C major) |
| --------------- | ----------------- |
| V7              | G – B – D – F     |
| vii°            | B – D – F         |
| vii⁷ (dim. 7th) | B – D – F – A♭    |

Three of V7's four pitches appear in vii°. The shared "DNA" is the leading tone (B in C major) plus
the tritone (B–F).

### The Implied Leading Tone

The leading tone can be **implied** rather than literally present:

- ii° in C major = D–F–A♭
- This omits the leading tone B entirely
- Yet D–F–A♭ is the close sibling of vii⁷ (B–D–F–A♭)
- The ear infers the leading tone and hears dominant function **Key analytical question:** Does a
  diminished chord contain — or imply — the leading tone of its target?

| Chord                                                | Leading tone status | Dominant function? |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------ |
| vii° or vii⁷ (has leading tone)                      | Present             | Yes                |
| ii° near dominant context (implies leading tone)     | Implied             | Yes (contextually) |
| Other diminished chords without leading-tone context | Absent              | No                 |

## Common Secondary Dominant Targets

In a major key, all non-tonic diatonic chords can be tonicized:

| Secondary Dominant | Target Chord  | Leading Tone Added (C major)           |
| ------------------ | ------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| V/ii               | ii (D minor)  | C#                                     |
| V/iii              | iii (E minor) | D#                                     |
| V/IV               | IV (F major)  | E♮ (already diatonic; adds C♮ as root) |
| V/V                | V (G major)   | F#                                     |
| V/vi               | vi (A minor)  | G#                                     |

**Most useful for hymn harmonization:** V/V → V and V/vi → vi (deceptive cadence setup)

## Practical Application: Hymn Reharmonization

### Finding Opportunities

1. Look for **ii → V** progressions — these are the easiest conversion (raise the third of ii)
2. Look for any chord with a bass note a fifth above a target chord
3. Look for places where a chromatic inflection would emphasize an important word in the text

### Checklist Before Adding a Secondary Dominant

- [ ] Does the chord contain the leading tone of the target?
- [ ] Are there cross relations with adjacent chords?
- [ ] Does the altered chord conflict with the melody?
- [ ] Does the text justify added harmonic intensity at this point?
- [ ] Have you adjusted surrounding harmony for smooth voice leading?

## Roman Numeral Quick Reference

```
V/V     → V         (e.g., D major → G major in C major)
V7/V    → V         (e.g., D7 → G major)
V/vi    → vi        (e.g., E major → A minor in C major)
V7/vi   → vi        (e.g., E7 → A minor)
V/ii    → ii        (e.g., A major → D minor in C major)
V7/IV   → IV        (e.g., C major → F major — rarely needed, IV already stable)
vii°/V  → V         (leading-tone diminished to dominant)
```
