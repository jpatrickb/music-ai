## The Five Essential Cadential Progressions

These five progressions form the core harmonic vocabulary for tonal music. Master them in every
major key before applying chromatic elaborations.

### Progression 1: Basic Cadence

```
I  —  IV  —  I⁶₄  —  V7  —  I
```

Soprano melody degrees: **8 – 8 – 8 – 7 – 8**

The fundamental authentic cadence. All other progressions below are modifications of this template.

**In C major:** C – F – C⁶₄ – G7 – C

---

### Progression 2: Deceptive Cadence

```
I  —  IV  —  I⁶₄  —  V7  —  vi
```

Soprano melody degrees: **8 – 8 – 8 – 7 – 8**

**What changed:** The final I is replaced by vi (the submediant, a minor chord).

**Why it works:** vi shares two of its three pitches with I:

- In C major: I = C–E–G; vi = A–C–E. The C and E are common tones.
- The ear expects resolution to I; the vi chord satisfies two-thirds of that expectation while
  redirecting to a new harmony. **Effect:** Surprise, incompleteness, a sense of turning away from
  the expected resolution. Can create a path toward a related key area.

**In C major:** C – F – C⁶₄ – G7 – A minor

---

### Progression 3: Secondary Dominant into Deceptive Cadence

```
I  —  IV  —  I⁶₄  —  V7/vi  —  vi
```

Soprano melody degrees: **8 – 8 – 8 – 9 – 8**

_(Note: melody degree 9 = scale degree 2, the ninth above tonic)_

**What changed:** V7 is replaced by V7/vi — a secondary dominant that points to vi as a temporary
tonic.

**Effect:** The deceptive cadence is now deliberate rather than surprising. The vi chord arrives as
the tonic of a brief key area rather than as an evasion. This is the gateway to modulation.

**In C major:** C – F – C⁶₄ – E7 – A minor

**Practical extension:** Continue the modulation by treating the vi as a new I:

- Start a new key area in A minor (or A major)
- Then modulate to F# major, then Eb major — cycling through the circle of fifths by descending
  thirds **LH note:** On the V7/vi chord (E7 in C major), LH plays E; top note of RH plays D (the
  7th of E7, = scale degree 9 of C major).

---

### Progression 4: Minor iv Chord

```
I  —  iv  —  I⁶₄  —  V7  —  I
```

Soprano melody degrees: **8 – 8 – 8 – 7 – 8**

**What changed:** The major IV chord is replaced by iv (minor subdominant, borrowed from the
parallel minor key).

**Effect:** Bittersweet, poignant, haunting. Creates emotional intensity by mixing a minor color
into the major context. Associated historically with Schubert.

**In C major:** C – F minor – C⁶₄ – G7 – C

**Voice-leading warning:** Always check for cross relations when moving from iv to the surrounding
major-key chords. The ♭3 of the iv chord (e.g., A♭ in F minor when in C major) must not create a
cross relation with A♮ in adjacent chords.

---

### Progression 5: Combined — Minor iv + Secondary Dominant into Deceptive Cadence

```
I  —  iv  —  I⁶₄  —  V7/vi  —  vi
```

Soprano melody degrees: **8 – 8 – 8 – 9 – 8**

**Combines:** the emotional coloring of the minor iv with the deliberate misdirection of the
secondary dominant deceptive cadence.

**In C major:** C – F minor – C⁶₄ – E7 – A minor

## The Deceptive Cadence Principle (Generalized)

The deceptive cadence (V7 → vi instead of V7 → I) is a special case of a general substitution
principle:

> **Any chord can be substituted by a chord a major or minor third lower.**

Because the substitute chord shares two common tones with the original, the ear accepts it while
experiencing a fresh color.

### Applying the Principle

**Step 1:** Choose any chord in the progression. **Step 2:** Substitute it with a chord whose root
is a major or minor 3rd lower. **Step 3:** Add secondary dominants to strengthen each substituted
chord if desired.

### Net Effect on a Piece

- Major chords become minor after substitution (and vice versa)
- The overall result has more minor quality — bittersweet character
- Use sparingly: the effect diminishes with overuse

### Adding Secondary Dominants to Substitutions

After applying deceptive-cadence substitutions, insert V7 chords:

- Immediately before each newly substituted chord, add its secondary dominant (V7 of that chord)
- **Caution:** The added secondary dominant may conflict with the existing melody; additional
  melodic adjustments may be required **Example:**
- Original: ... C major chord ...
- After substitution: ... A minor chord ...
- After adding secondary dominant: ... E7 → A minor ...

## Soprano Melody Variants

Once the basic voicings are secure, practice each progression with alternate soprano lines:

| Pattern       | Melody degrees    |
| ------------- | ----------------- |
| Primary       | 8 – 8 – 8 – 7 – 8 |
| Third variant | 3 – 4 – 3 – 4 – 3 |
| Fifth variant | 5 – 6 – 5 – 5 – 5 |

Apply all three variants to all five progressions in every major key.

## Voicing Reference (C Major)

All examples in keyboard style (LH = root, RH = three voices, soprano on top):

```
I⁶₄:     LH: G    RH (T-A-S): G–C–E
V7:       LH: G    RH (T-A-S): F–B–D   (or F–D–B with soprano on D)
i:        LH: C    RH (T-A-S): C–E–G
vi:       LH: A    RH (T-A-S): A–C–E
V7/vi:    LH: E    RH (T-A-S): B–D#–G# (E major with minor 7th = E–G#–B–D)
iv:       LH: F    RH (T-A-S): F–Ab–C
```

## Circle of Thirds Chain Modulation

Using Progression 3 as a springboard, one can modulate through keys by descending thirds:

```
Start in C Major → land on A minor (vi)
→ Treat A minor as i; play I–IV–I⁶₄–V7–I in A major
→ Now land on F# major (as VI of A)
→ Treat F# as I; play I–IV–I⁶₄–V7–I in F# major
→ Land on D# / Eb major
→ ... eventually returns to C major
```

This is an efficient way to practice all keys while exploring chromatic voice leading.
