## Overview: Modal Mixture

**Modal mixture** (also called **modal borrowing**) means importing a chord from the parallel minor
(or parallel major) key into the home key.

- In a **major key**: borrow chords from the parallel natural minor scale
- The major and minor versions of a key share the same dominant (V) — this is what makes borrowing
  coherent **Theoretical basis:** Since both C major and C minor use G major as their dominant, a C
  major piece can "borrow" minor-key chords without destabilizing the tonal center. The dominant
  still points home.

**Schubert's mastery:** Schubert mixed major and minor modes so thoroughly that his major-key works
carry pervasive minor-tinged melancholy. His aphorism from this course:

> "When Schubert is in a minor key, it is sad. But when Schubert is in a major key, it's even
> sadder."

## The Minor iv Chord

### Construction

In a major key, the IV chord is naturally major. The **minor iv** chord lowers the third of IV by a
half step, producing a minor triad.

| Key     | IV (major)        | iv (minor, borrowed) |
| ------- | ----------------- | -------------------- |
| C major | F–A–C (F major)   | F–A♭–C (F minor)     |
| G major | C–E–G (C major)   | C–E♭–G (C minor)     |
| D major | G–B–D (G major)   | G–B♭–D (G minor)     |
| F major | B♭–D–F (B♭ major) | B♭–D♭–F (B♭ minor)   |
| E major | A–C#–E (A major)  | A–C–E (A minor)      |

**Roman numeral:** iv (lowercase = minor quality) **Origin:** The iv chord as it naturally appears
in the parallel natural minor key.

### Harmonic Function and Effect

- Replaces IV in the subdominant function position
- **Sound:** Poignant, haunting, bittersweet — "guaranteed to be a tear jerker"
- Creates emotional intensity through the unexpected minor quality within a major-key context
- Strongest effect when associated with text about struggle, longing, or sorrow **Famous examples:**
- Beethoven, Emperor Concerto No. 5, 2nd movement (E minor chord in major context)
- Rachmaninov, Piano Concerto No. 2, 2nd movement (A minor in E major)
- Eric Carmen, "All by Myself" (G minor in D major — directly borrowed from Rachmaninov)
- LDS hymn "Love One Another" (B♭ minor in F major)

### Voice Leading Rules

**Critical:** Do NOT create cross relations.

The minor iv chord contains a ♭3 (e.g., A♭ in F minor when in C major). This ♭3 must not appear
alongside its natural version (A♮) in an adjacent voice in an adjacent chord.

**Common problem:** Moving from minor iv back to I or to I⁶₄:

- F minor (F–A♭–C) → C major⁶₄ (G–C–E) → the A♭ in the preceding chord must be resolved and not
  conflict with A♮ in the new chord
- Keep the A♭ in the same voice and resolve it smoothly

### How and When to Use It

**Step-by-step process:**

1. Locate IV chords in the piece
2. Ask: would a minor iv work here harmonically?
3. Does it change the melody? (If so, avoid when accompanying a congregation unrehearsed)
4. Check for cross relations with adjacent chords
5. Does the text justify the emotional weight of a minor chord? **Usage guidance:**

- Use on one verse of a multi-verse hymn, not all of them
- Match to text content — avoid using at joyful textual moments
- Use sparingly — strong effect that diminishes with overuse

### Common Progressions

```
I  —  iv  —  I⁶₄  —  V7  —  I          (basic substitution)
I  —  iv  —  I⁶₄  —  V7/vi  —  vi      (combined with deceptive cadence)
I  —  IV  —  iv  —  I⁶₄  —  V7  —  I  (IV then iv for extra color)
```

## The Flat-VII Chord (♭VII)

### Construction

The ♭VII chord is a major triad built on the lowered seventh scale degree (one half step below the
natural leading tone).

| Key     | V (dominant) | ♭VII (lowered 7th) |
| ------- | ------------ | ------------------ |
| C major | G major      | B♭ major           |
| G major | D major      | F major            |
| D major | A major      | C major            |
| F major | C major      | E♭ major           |

**Roman numeral:** ♭VII (major triad — uppercase — on lowered 7th degree) **Origin:** Borrowed from
parallel Mixolydian or parallel natural minor.

### Harmonic Character

More distant than secondary dominants or augmented sixths — like "importing from Puerto Rico"
compared to the home state. Creates an exotic, unexpected color. Best used in arrangements that
already have other chromatic alterations, not as a standalone surprise.

**Famous example:** "Come, Come Ye Saints" (arrangement at 3:49) — ♭VII chord gives an energizing,
empowering effect.

### Required Setup Conditions

The ♭VII chord substitutes for the V chord at a phrase ending, but only when a specific melodic
condition is met:

**Condition:** The melody must be on **scale degree 5 of the V chord** (i.e., the fifth of the
dominant triad).

**Example in G major:**

- V chord = D major (D–F#–A)
- Scale degree 5 of D major = A
- If the melody note is A, the condition is met
- Replace D major with F major (♭VII of G major)
- The melody note A fits into F major as its major third ✓

### Resolution

```
... V chord (with scale degree 5 in melody) ...
→ ♭VII chord (held ~2 beats)
→ V7 (return to original dominant)
→ I
```

The ♭VII is "far from the key center" and needs to return through V7.

**Voice leading tip:** Add a 9th to the ♭VII chord — sweetens the harmony.

### Full Formula

```
[phrase ending on V with scale degree 5 in soprano]
→ ♭VII  →  V7  →  I
```

In G major: A in melody → F major → D7 → G major

## The Flat-III Chord (♭III)

### Construction

The ♭III chord is a major triad built on the lowered third scale degree.

| Key     | iii (minor) | ♭III (major, borrowed) |
| ------- | ----------- | ---------------------- |
| C major | E minor     | E♭ major               |
| G major | B minor     | B♭ major               |
| D major | F# minor    | F major                |
| F major | A minor     | A♭ major               |

**Roman numeral:** ♭III (major triad on lowered 3rd degree) **Origin:** Same modal mixture as ♭VII —
borrowed from parallel minor.

### Relationship to ♭VII

These two chords are parallel substitutions for the V chord at phrase endings, distinguished only by
which melody note is present:

| Melody note over V chord    | Substitution |
| --------------------------- | ------------ |
| Scale degree 5 (fifth of V) | ♭VII         |
| Scale degree 1 (root of V)  | ♭III         |

Both hold their respective positions, then resolve back to V (or V7/V9).

### Required Setup Conditions

**Condition:** The melody must be on **scale degree 1 of the V chord** (i.e., the root of the
dominant triad).

**Example in C major:**

- V chord = G major (G–B–D)
- Scale degree 1 of G major = G
- If the melody note is G, the condition is met
- Replace G major with E♭ major (♭III of C major)
- The melody note G fits into E♭ major as... the major third? Actually G is the major third of E♭. ✓
  **Famous example:** "God of Our Fathers, Whose Almighty Hand" — the ♭III chord appears in line 3
  (one of very few hymns in the LDS hymnal to use it). The instructor describes it as the best
  harmonically interesting moment in that hymn.

**Example in G major:** "I Need Thee Every Hour," measure 8 at "ford": D major chord (V in G major)
with G in the melody → substitute B♭ major (♭III of G major).

### Resolution

```
[V chord with scale degree 1 of V in soprano]
→ ♭III (held ~2 beats)
→ V (or V9)
→ I
```

In G major: G in melody over D major → B♭ major → D9 → G major

**Voice leading tip:** Add 9ths to smooth over the strong harmonic shift. The ♭III creates a more
dramatic shift than ♭VII — 9ths are especially important here.

**Leading tone consideration:** The V chord's leading tone (C# in D major) may need to be
temporarily omitted when moving to ♭III. If C# is replaced by C♮, a new leading tone to B♭ is
created — but watch for parallel leading tones.

## Comparison: The Three Borrowed Chords

| Chord | Built on       | Replaces | Melody condition                    | Emotional character              |
| ----- | -------------- | -------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| iv    | ♭3 (minor 3rd) | IV       | No condition — replaces IV directly | Poignant, sad, bittersweet       |
| ♭VII  | ♭7 (min. 7th)  | V        | Scale degree 5 in melody over V     | Exotic, empowering, distant      |
| ♭III  | ♭3 (minor 3rd) | V        | Scale degree 1 in melody over V     | Dramatic, striking, very distant |

**Strength of effect (weakest → strongest):** iv < ♭VII < ♭III

## The Minor iv as a Pivot Chord for Modulation

_(See also [05-pivot-chord-modulation.md](05-pivot-chord-modulation.md))_

Schubert exploits the minor iv chord's dual identity for **third-relation modulations**:

- The minor iv belongs simultaneously to the parallel major and parallel minor of its key
- Since both parallel keys share the same dominant, the minor iv can be interpreted in multiple
  tonal contexts
- This makes it an effective **common-chord pivot** between distantly related keys **Schubert
  example:** "Der Hirt auf dem Felsen" ("The Shepherd on the Rock")
- G major/G minor → B♭ major
- The minor iv chord (C minor) functions in both key areas **Third-relation modulations using minor
  iv:**
- A chord that is iv in one key may be ii, iv, or vi in another key
- Most effective for modulations by major or minor thirds (chromatic mediant relations)

## Universal Voice-Leading Rules for All Borrowed Chords

1. **No cross relations** — check every borrowed chord against all voices in adjacent chords
2. **Minimal voice movement** — do not leap just because the chord is chromatic
3. **Match to text** — use only where the emotional character fits the words
4. **Use sparingly** — one verse, not all verses; one placement per section
5. **Add 9ths to smooth transitions** — especially for ♭VII and ♭III
6. **Do not alter the melody** when accompanying a congregation unrehearsed
