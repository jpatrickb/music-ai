# Common-Tone Modulation

> **Source context:** BYU MUSIC 264/265, Keyboard Foundations 1 & 2 (Dr. Scott Holden)
> **Prerequisite:** [05-pivot-chord-modulation.md](05-pivot-chord-modulation.md)

---

## Definition

**Common-tone modulation** pivots on a single melodic pitch shared between the old key and the new
key — not a shared chord (as in pivot-chord modulation), but a shared _note_. The melody's final
pitch in the old key is reinterpreted as a different scale degree in the new key, causing a sudden,
dramatic key change.

**Metaphor used in class:** Pivot-chord modulation = nutritious vegetables; common-tone modulation =
starburst candy / dessert. It is striking and delicious but not a substitute for mastering
pivot-chord technique.

---

## How It Differs from Pivot-Chord Modulation

| Feature              | Pivot-Chord Modulation    | Common-Tone Modulation       |
| -------------------- | ------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Pivot element        | A full chord in both keys | A single melodic pitch       |
| Seam visibility      | Camouflaged — smooth      | Visible — abrupt, dazzling   |
| Harmonic preparation | V7 → I framework          | None required                |
| Effect               | Natural, logical flow     | Dramatic surprise            |
| Typical usage        | Structural modulation     | End-of-verse effect in songs |

---

## The Mechanism

The standard common-tone modulation works as follows:

1. The melody lands on its final note of a phrase (most commonly scale degree 1)
2. Instead of resolving to the expected tonic chord, a **different major chord** is substituted
3. That substitute chord contains the melody's final note as its **third (mediant)**
4. The music continues in the new key for a full additional verse **The reinterpretation:** The
   melody note does not move — it is physically the same pitch. But its function changes: from the
   root (scale degree 1) of the old tonic, it becomes the third (mediant) of the new tonic.

---

## The Three Cases by Melody Scale Degree

### Case 1: Melody on Scale Degree 1 (most common)

> New key is a **minor sixth up** from the old key (= major third down, enharmonically equivalent).

**Formula:** Old tonic → new tonic a minor 6th higher (e.g., C → A♭)

| Old key  | Melody note       | New key  | Melody note as chord tone  |
| -------- | ----------------- | -------- | -------------------------- |
| C major  | C (scale deg. 1)  | A♭ major | C = major third of A♭      |
| A♭ major | A♭ (scale deg. 1) | E major  | A♭ = G# = major third of E |
| G major  | G (scale deg. 1)  | E♭ major | G = major third of E♭      |
| D major  | D (scale deg. 1)  | B♭ major | D = major third of B♭      |
| F major  | F (scale deg. 1)  | D♭ major | F = major third of D♭      |

**Key relationship:** The old tonic and new tonic are a **chromatic mediant** (major third) apart —
one of the most dramatic remote key relationships in tonal music.

### Case 2: Melody on Scale Degree 5 (rare)

> New key is a **minor third up** from the old key.

**Formula:** Old tonic → new tonic a minor 3rd higher (e.g., C → E♭)

| Old key | Melody note      | New key  | Melody note as chord tone |
| ------- | ---------------- | -------- | ------------------------- |
| C major | G (scale deg. 5) | E♭ major | G = major third of E♭     |
| G major | D (scale deg. 5) | B♭ major | D = major third of B♭     |

### Case 3: Melody on Scale Degree 3 (very rare)

> New key is a **major sixth up** from the old key.

**Formula:** Old tonic → new tonic a major 6th higher (e.g., C → A)

| Old key | Melody note      | New key | Melody note as chord tone |
| ------- | ---------------- | ------- | ------------------------- |
| C major | E (scale deg. 3) | A major | E = fifth of A            |

_(Note: in this case the common tone becomes the fifth, not the third, of the new chord.)_

## Enharmonic Equivalence

Common-tone modulations frequently cross an enharmonic seam:

**Example:** A♭ major → E major

- Final melody note: A♭ (scale degree 1 of A♭ major)
- New chord: E major (E–G#–B)
- A♭ = G# enharmonically → A♭ is the same pitch as G#, the major third of E major The enharmonic
  reinterpretation is what allows the modulation to span a seemingly large distance. On the piano
  the note doesn't change; only its harmonic spelling and function change.

## Voice-Leading Guidelines

### No Preparation Required

Unlike pivot-chord modulation, no secondary dominant or transitional chord is needed before the
common-tone substitution. The substitute chord appears directly in place of the expected tonic.

### Add the Ninth to the New Tonic

**Explicitly recommended:** Add the 9th to the new tonic chord immediately after the modulation.
This:

- Softens the shock of parallel chromaticism between keys
- Provides a cushion against harsh voice motion (e.g., when E♭ in the old key moves to E♮ in the new
  key)
- Sweetens the arrival **Example:** A♭ major → E major: play E major with added 9th (E–G#–B–F#)
  instead of bare E major triad.

### The Common Tone Stays Put

The melody note (the common tone) does not move at the modulation point. The surrounding harmony
changes beneath it. The melody note's held or restated pitch is the anchor of the transition.

### "Dress Up" the New Key

After the modulation, the new key should be harmonically richer than the preceding material — more
elaborate voicing, added ninths, secondary dominants — to justify and warrant the exotic key change.

---

## Practical Procedure (Hymn Arranging Context)

1. Identify the final melody note of the verse (almost always scale degree 1)
2. Ask: "What major chords contain this note as their third (mediant)?"
3. Avoid minor chords as the new tonic (unless deliberately arranging a minor verse)
4. Choose the option that moves farthest from the original key for maximum dramatic effect
   - If in C major (C in melody): A♭ is more interesting than F (F is too close to C major)
5. Add the 9th to the new tonic chord
6. Continue with a full verse in the new key, with richer harmonization

## Canonical Musical Examples

### Schumann, "Widmung" (Dedication), Op. 25 No. 1 (1840)

- **Modulation:** A♭ major → E major (B section, approximately 0:35)
- **Mechanism:** Final melody note A♭ = G# = mediant of E major
- **Text painting:** The word _Schmerz_ ("pain") foreshadows the modulation; on the last line "pain"
  becomes "rest" and the rhythm relaxes into triplets
- **Analysis:** This is the canonical art-music exemplar of common-tone modulation; exact match to
  Case 1 (scale degree 1 in A♭ → third of E)

### Bill Evans, "Young and Foolish" (recording)

- **Modulation:** C major → A♭ major (at approximately 3:15–3:16)
- **Mechanism:** C in melody (scale degree 1 of C) = mediant of A♭ major
- **Note:** At 4:05, Evans uses a _different_, more sophisticated modulation described as "a
  Thanksgiving feast" — this is NOT a common-tone modulation but is worth studying

### Hymn Example: "I Stand All Amazed" (A♭ major)

- Final melody note at the word "me": A♭ (scale degree 1)
- Substitute E major (A♭ = G# = mediant of E major)
- Add 9th to E major for smoothness (F# added)

## Key Relationships at a Glance

For the most common case (melody on scale degree 1), the common-tone modulation always produces a
**chromatic mediant** relationship — old and new tonics are a major third apart:

```
Starting key:   ...  Ab  Eb  Bb  F   C   G   D   A   E   B  ...
New key (up m6):...  E   B   F#  Db  Ab  Eb  Bb  F   C   G  ...
```

Each new key is three steps counterclockwise on the circle of fifths from the original (= a major
third lower = a minor sixth higher).

## Comparison: Modulation Types

| Technique              | Shared element         | Distance               | Effect             | Preparation       |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------ | ----------------- |
| Pivot chord (iii)      | Full chord             | ±2 circle steps        | Smooth             | 8-chord framework |
| Pivot chord (minor iv) | Full chord (borrowed)  | Any (not tritone)      | Smooth to moderate | 8-chord framework |
| Common-tone            | Single melody note     | Major third (typical)  | Abrupt, dramatic   | None              |
| Aug. 6th chord         | Enharmonic equivalence | Half step or major 3rd | Sudden             | Cadential setup   |
