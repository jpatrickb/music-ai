# Jazz Piano Styles

---

## 1. Bebop Piano (1940s–1950s)

**Key figures:** Bud Powell, Thelonious Monk, Barry Harris, Oscar Peterson

### Core Concept: Liberation of the Right Hand

Bebop piano separates the two hands' roles completely: the left hand maintains sparse, unpredictable
harmonic support ("comping") that never gets in the way of the right hand's long, virtuosic melodic
lines.

---

### Left Hand: Comping

**What comping is:** Harmonic accompaniment consisting of chord hits placed on unexpected rhythmic
positions — not a steady pulse, but a responsive dialogue with the melodic line. The left hand
punctuates; it does not keep time.

**Shell voicings (stripped chords):**

- Root + 3rd only
- Root + 7th only
- 3rd + 7th only (the "guide tones" — most harmonically specific) The 3rd and 7th together define
  chord quality (major vs. minor vs. dominant) without the root. The bass player covers the root.

**Rootless voicings (bebop standard):**

Two standard positions for ii–V–I in C major:

| Chord | Type A voicing | Type B voicing |
| ----- | -------------- | -------------- |
| Dm7   | C–F–A          | F–A–C–E        |
| G7    | B–F–A–C        | F–A–B–E        |
| Cmaj7 | B–E–G          | E–G–B–D        |

Both move by minimal voice-leading steps (semitones or whole tones).

---

### Right Hand: Bebop Lines

Long, flowing single-note lines in eighth notes, built from:

**The bebop dominant scale:** G Mixolydian + added chromatic passing tone between ♭7 and root: G – A
– B – C – D – E – F – F♯ – G

The added F♯ ensures that **chord tones fall on the beat** when descending in eighth notes — a
crucial mechanical property.

**The bebop major scale:** C major + added chromatic between 5th and 6th: C – D – E – F – G – G♯ – A
– B – C

**Chromatic approach notes:** Arriving at a chord tone from a half step below (from beneath) or
above (from above).

**Encircling (enclosure):** Surrounding a target tone with the note above and the note below before
landing:

- Target E: play F → E♭ → E
- Or E♭ → F♯ → E **Swing feel:** Notated as two equal eighth notes; performed as a long-short
  triplet subdivision (approximately dotted-eighth + sixteenth in feel, but more even at fast
  tempos). **Laying back:** Playing slightly behind the metronomic center to create tension against
  the rhythm section.

---

### ii–V–I: The Structural DNA

Virtually all bebop tunes are sequences of ii–V–I progressions in multiple keys. Harmonic rhythm
moves quickly — often two chords per measure or faster. Standard in B♭ major:

```
Cm7 – F7 – B♭maj7 (or B♭6)
```

The V chord almost always carries alterations: ♭9, ♯9, ♭13, or ♯11. Plain dominant 7ths sound
underdressed.

---

### Thelonious Monk's Specific Style

**Rhythmic displacement:** Notes placed on unexpected beats; rests where others would play; plays
where others would rest. The "clunky" quality is deeply intentional.

**Clusters:** Minor-second and major-second clusters as harmonic color — adjacent semitones played
simultaneously (e.g., C + D♭ as a "crunch") that resolve.

**Tritone substitutions:** Structurally, not just as passing colors. G7 → D♭7 (both share the
tritone F–B/F–C♭).

**Unusual voicings:** 7th on the bottom, root on top; or 5th omitted with remaining tones in
unexpected spacing. Chord quality deliberately obscured.

**Stride remnants:** Occasional left-hand stride patterns quoted ironically.

## 2. Cool Jazz and Modal Jazz (1950s–1960s)

**Key figures:** Bill Evans, Miles Davis, McCoy Tyner, John Coltrane quartet

### Bill Evans: Voicing Architecture

**Quartal harmony (voicings in 4ths):** Instead of stacking 3rds, stack perfect 4ths (and tritones):

- Traditional Cmaj7: C–E–G–B
- Quartal: C–F–B–E or D–G–C–F (all perfect 4ths) These voicings are "pandiatonic" — they don't
  clearly imply a single chord quality and can float over multiple harmonic contexts.

**Cluster voicings (voicings in 2nds):** Close-position voicings built from major and minor 2nds.
Dense, colorful clusters that Evans uses for lyrical effect. A maj9 chord might place the 9th
immediately adjacent to the root.

**Inner-voice movement (Evans's most distinctive contribution):** When moving chord to chord, keep
common tones stationary; move remaining voices by the smallest possible interval. Example over
ii–V–I in G major:

| Chord  | Voicing                                   |
| ------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| Am7    | A–C–E–G                                   |
| D7(♭9) | A–C–F♯–G (only E→F♯ moves, by whole step) |
| Gmaj7  | B–D–F♯–G or G–B–D–F♯                      |

The result: a "woven" texture where melody, inner voices, and harmony move independently but
cohesively.

**Locked-hands / block chord technique:** The melody plays in the right hand; the left hand plays a
chord voicing that exactly mirrors the right-hand rhythm — both hands locked in rhythmic lockstep.
Inner voices fill between the melody and bass. Creates an orchestral "piano as choir" sound.

---

### Modal Jazz

Miles Davis's _Kind of Blue_ (1959) established the principle: instead of rapid chord changes, a
single mode is sustained for many bars.

**"So What" vamp (D Dorian):** D Dorian: D–E–F–G–A–B–C–D (natural minor with raised 6th)

**The "So What" voicing** (a quartal chord used as the tonic sound):

- Left hand: D–G–C–F (stacked perfect 4ths)
- Right hand: A–E (perfect 5th on top)
- Total: D–G–C–F–A–E — all white notes (when in D) **Common modal centers:** | Mode | Construction |
  Character | |---|---|---| | Dorian | Natural minor + ♮6 | Dark but not tragic; most common in
  modal jazz | | Mixolydian | Major + ♭7 | Open, dominant-feel; blues-adjacent | | Lydian | Major +
  ♯4 | Bright, hovering, ethereal | | Phrygian | Natural minor + ♭2 | Tense, Spanish/flamenco
  quality |

---

### McCoy Tyner: Power and Pentatonics

**Left-hand comping:** Percussive, resonant intervals — perfect 4ths, perfect 5ths, and octaves in
the bass register. Creates a drone-like, powerful foundation. On a D minor vamp:

- LH alternates or sustains: D–A (perfect 5th) or A–D–G (stacked 4ths, lower register) **Pentatonic
  scales as primary melodic material:**
- Minor pentatonic: root–♭3–4–5–♭7 (e.g., D–F–G–A–C)
- Major pentatonic: root–2–3–5–6 (e.g., C–D–E–G–A)
- Anhemitonic (no semitones): both major and minor pentatonic qualify — their open quality is why
  Tyner favors them for extended passages **Pentatonic superimposition:** Playing a pentatonic scale
  rooted on a different scale degree than the underlying chord — creates "outside" tensions. A
  pentatonic from a 4th, tritone, or minor 3rd above the root creates systematic outside/inside
  movement.

**The "Tyner crunch":** At climactic moments, clusters of left-hand quartal voicings driven with
heavy forearm weight while the right hand runs parallel or contrary pentatonic passages.
Intentionally percussive.

---

### Reharmonization (modal and cool era)

- **Tritone substitution:** D♭7 for G7 — both share the tritone F–B/F–C♭
- **Back-cycling:** Moving backward around the circle of fifths (G7 → C7 → F7 → B♭7) as an approach
  chain to create forward momentum
- **Modal interchange:** Using ♭VII or ♭III borrowed from parallel minor in a major key context
- **Pedal point reharmonization:** Sustaining a bass note while upper voices move through unrelated
  chords **Space and silence (Evans):** Evans makes silence structurally significant. States a
  phrase; rests for an equivalent duration; answers. The space is not absence — it is where the
  listener's ear extends the phrase. Influenced by Ravel and Debussy.

## 3. Afro-Cuban Jazz Piano

**Key figures:** Chucho Valdés, Chick Corea, Irakere ensemble

### The Clave

Clave is the rhythmic spine — all piano patterns must align with or complement it.

**Son clave (3–2), written as 8th-note positions across 2 bars of 4/4:**

```
Bar 1 (the "3"):  X . . X . X . .   (beats 1, 2-and, 4)
Bar 2 (the "2"):  . X . X . . . .   (beats 2, 3)
```

**Son clave (2–3):** Same pattern, bars reversed.

**Rumba clave (3–2):** Bar 1's "4" hit shifts to "4-and" — the "heavier" clave creating more
tension.

**Critical rule:** Playing a 3–2 clave pattern against a 2–3 context is a fundamental error. The
pianist must internalize which clave direction is in effect.

---

### Montuno Pattern

The defining Afro-Cuban piano pattern: a repeating two-bar arpeggiated chord figure in the right
hand, played continuously.

**Structural characteristics:**

- Two bars long, looped
- Upper register (above middle C)
- Arpeggiated — chord tones spread rhythmically rather than struck simultaneously
- Highly syncopated — anticipates beats by an eighth note **The anticipation:** Notes arrive on the
  "and" of the preceding beat rather than on the beat itself. This "tumbling forward" feel is
  essential — it distinguishes Afro-Cuban feel from straight jazz time.

**Classic montuno on C7 → F7 (simplified right hand):**

```
Bar 1 (C7): "and of 4" (from prev.) → beat 2 → and-of-3 → beat 4
Bar 2 (F7): same anticipation pattern transposed to F
```

Two-note voicing fragments (10ths, 6ths, or 3rds) rather than full chords — allows rapid movement.

---

### Tumbaó (Bass Pattern)

The Afro-Cuban bass — fundamentally different from North American walking bass:

**Key distinction:** The bass anticipates the downbeat. The root appears on the "and of 4" of the
preceding bar, making beat 1 often silent or lightly marked.

**Basic tumbaó in C:**

- "and of 4": C (anticipated root)
- Beat 3: G (5th) or another chord tone This anticipated bass is the primary rhythmic marker
  distinguishing Afro-Cuban from jazz bass. In jazz, the bass walks evenly (1–2–3–4); in Afro-Cuban,
  the bass anticipates.

---

### Chord Voicings in Afro-Cuban Context

- Dominant 7♭9 and 7♯9 are standard (C7♭9 = C–E–B♭–D♭; C7♯9 = C–E–B♭–D♯)
- Open 5th and octave bass notes with upper structure in right hand
- Two-note interval stabs in montuno (not full 4-note voicings)
- **Chucho Valdés / Irakere style:** Bebop rootless voicings integrated into the montuno tradition —
  harmonically complex but clave-aligned

## 4. Fusion and Contemporary Jazz Piano (1970s–present)

**Key figures:** Herbie Hancock, Chick Corea, Keith Jarrett, Robert Glasper (see also Neo-Soul)

### Herbie Hancock: Vamp and Modal Ostinato

**"Cantaloupe Island" texture:**

- Static two-chord vamp: Fm7 (three bars) → D♭7 (one bar) → D7 (one bar)
- Left hand plays a rhythmically assertive vamp — not sparse bebop comping but a locked groove
- Right hand plays melodic ideas over the static harmony
- Modal approach: F Dorian/Aeolian without directed harmonic motion **Electric piano influence on
  acoustic style:** The Rhodes and Wurlitzer reward lighter touch and more emphasis on attack
  transient. Hancock's acoustic piano after his electric period shows lighter touch, more staccato
  articulation, and greater emphasis on rhythmic placement over legato phrasing.

---

### Chick Corea: Spain and Flamenco Influence

**"Spain" (1972) structure:**

- Introduction quotes Rodrigo's "Concierto de Aranjuez" — direct classical borrowing
- Flamenco Phrygian harmony: i → ♭VII → ♭VI → V (Andalusian cadence)
- In A minor: Am → G → F → E7 (E7 is major dominant in otherwise Phrygian context) **Staccato
  articulation:** Unlike Evans's sustained, pedaled sound, Corea attacks crisply and releases
  quickly. Minimal sustain pedal. Clear separation between notes. Rhythmic incisiveness over legato.

**Corea's voicings:**

- Open 5ths (C–G) or 4ths in the bass
- Upper-structure triads: a triad in the RH "outside" the LH root
  - E major triad over C bass = C7(♯9) or C Lydian dominant color
- Sus4 voicings: C–G–B♭–F (11th chord, suspended, no 3rd) **Rasgueado-inspired technique:** Rapid
  repeated-note figures in the right hand (same pitch, repeated by alternating fingers: 4-3-2-1).
  Simulates guitar rasgueado. Corea uses these against a left hand that marks time with staccato
  octaves.

---

### Keith Jarrett: Solo Concert Style

**Structural approach:** Improvised pieces built with the architecture of classical compositions —
introduction, thematic development, climax, recapitulation. A single piece may last 20–26 minutes
with clear internal formal logic.

**Gospel influence:** Left hand plays gospel-style alternating bass/chord patterns (root on beat 1,
chord on beat 3, or full "church" left hand). Right hand sings gospel-inflected melodies.

**Classical references:** Baroque counterpoint (two independent voices interweaving); Romantic-era
chord progressions (Brahms-like thick voicings); Impressionist textures (Debussian whole-tone or
pentatonic washes).

---

## 5. Stride as Full Harmonic System (Advanced)

The fully developed Harlem stride pianists (James P. Johnson, Fats Waller, Art Tatum) conceived of
stride as a complete orchestral substitute.

### The Four-Layer Texture

1. **Bass line (bass/tuba equivalent):** LH, beats 1 and 3 — single notes in low register, octave
   below middle C or lower
2. **Chord stabs (brass/rhythm):** LH, beats 2 and 4 — mid-range chord voicings, struck and released
3. **Inner accompaniment:** Right-hand inner voices filling between melody notes
4. **Melody (trumpet/voice):** RH, upper register The left hand executes layers 1 and 2 alternately
   on every beat — physically demanding jumps of 2–3 octaves in a fraction of a second at 200 BPM.

### Walking Tenths

Left hand plays a 10th interval (root + 3rd an octave up) as a unit, voice-led in parallel motion
through a progression:

- C major 10th: C2 + E3
- Moving to F: F2 + A3
- Chromatic approach: B1 + D♯3 → C2 + E3 Creates a full bass line with an independent harmony note
  simultaneously. Art Tatum's mastery: walking tenths in the LH while the RH plays melody and
  improvised counterpoint.
