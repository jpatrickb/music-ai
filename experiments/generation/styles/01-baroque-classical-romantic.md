# Baroque, Classical, and Romantic Piano Styles

---

## 1. Baroque Era (approx. 1600–1750)

**Key composers:** J.S. Bach, Domenico Scarlatti, George Frideric Handel

### Core Concept: Voices, Not Chords

Baroque keyboard writing treats the instrument as an ensemble of independent melodic lines. There is
no "melody hand" and "accompaniment hand" — both hands carry equally important melodic voices. Each
voice has its own rhythmic profile, melodic contour, and motivic identity.

**Standard voice textures:**

- **Two-voice:** Each hand carries one line. Neither is accompaniment. Bach's Two-Part Inventions
  (BWV 772–786) are the canonical example — the subject introduced in one hand is immediately
  answered in the other while the first continues with a countersubject.
- **Three-voice:** The most common fugal texture. The middle voice is often shared between hands,
  sometimes requiring the left hand to ascend above the right.
- **Four-voice:** Rarer in keyboard writing; requires constant hand-position management to
  distribute four independent lines across two hands. **Imitation:** The defining structural
  principle. A motive (short melodic cell) introduced in one voice is repeated exactly — or in
  inversion, retrograde, augmentation, or diminution — in another voice a few beats later.

---

### Left Hand Patterns

- **Independent contrapuntal voice:** The left hand is a fully melodic voice equal in importance to
  the right. It shares motives, carries the subject in fugues, and interweaves with the right hand.
- **Walking bass (quarter or eighth notes):** In dance movements, a steady bass in equal note values
  provides harmonic clarity.
- **Suspensions:** A pitch held over from a consonant position into a dissonant beat, then resolving
  stepwise. Chains of 7–6 or 4–3 suspensions are a stock cadential approach. Notated as tied notes
  across the barline.
- **Sequences:** A pattern repeated at successive pitch levels. The bass often drives the sequence,
  with the descending-fifth sequence (circle of fifths) being most common.

---

### Right Hand Patterns

- Scale runs in sixteenth notes (sequence episodes)
- Parallel thirds or sixths as inner-voice filling
- Cadential trill on the penultimate note before a tonic arrival
- Ornamental flourishes specific to the dance form in use

---

### Ornamentation

All ornaments are **structural and notated** (not improvised), begin **on the beat**, and start from
the upper auxiliary note in most cases.

| Ornament                     | Symbol                | Description                                                                                                                                |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Trill                        | `tr` or `~~~`         | Rapid alternation, main note ↔ upper neighbor. In Baroque: starts on upper note. Ends with a turn unless marked otherwise.                 |
| Mordent (lower)              | `♩` with line through | Main → lower neighbor → main. Fast, percussive. Used on downbeats.                                                                         |
| Upper mordent / Pralltriller | `~~`                  | Main → upper → main. Rapid, 3 notes. Mid-phrase decoration.                                                                                |
| Turn                         | `∞` or S-shape        | Upper → main → lower → main. Can be placed on or after the written note.                                                                   |
| Appoggiatura                 | Small grace note      | Played **on the beat**, taking half (or more) the main note's value. Dissonant on the beat, resolves to main note. Always long in Baroque. |

---

### Harmonic Language

- **Figured bass logic:** The bass voice determines harmonic identity. Chord quality derives from
  intervals above the bass note.
- **Sequences as harmonic motion:** The descending-fifth sequence traverses large harmonic distances
  quickly. Descending-step sequences often use parallel 6/3 chords (fauxbourdon). Both are
  explicitly patterned and intended to sound that way — repetition drives momentum.
- **Suspension chains:** 7–6, 4–3, or 9–8 chains create forward harmonic pull at cadences.

---

### Dance Forms and Rhythmic Characters

| Dance              | Meter       | Tempo    | Characteristic                                                                                  |
| ------------------ | ----------- | -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Allemande          | 4/4         | Moderate | Short upbeat (3 sixteenth notes); continuous 16th-note motion throughout                        |
| Courante (French)  | 3/2         | Stately  | Hemiola between 3/2 and 6/4, especially at cadences                                             |
| Corrente (Italian) | 3/4 or 3/8  | Fast     | Continuous running 16ths; less rhythmic complexity than French courante                         |
| Sarabande          | 3/4         | Slow     | Stress on **beat 2** (often a dotted quarter); highly ornamented; harmonically richest movement |
| Gigue              | 6/8 or 12/8 | Fast     | Fugal; second half often uses inverted subject; dotted rhythms, angular leaps                   |
| Bourrée            | 4/4 or 2/2  | Brisk    | Quarter-note upbeat on beat 4; energetic, dance-like                                            |

---

### Dynamics

**Terraced dynamics:** Sudden shifts between forte and piano at phrase boundaries — not gradual
crescendos within phrases. This reflects the harpsichord's touch-insensitive mechanism. Echo effects
(phrase forte → same phrase piano) are characteristic of Handel and Scarlatti.

**Note:** Bach's clavichord music can produce subtle touch-sensitive dynamics; the purely terraced
approach is instrument-specific.

---

### Voicing Habits

- Close position preferred; notes cluster within an octave or tenth
- Voice crossing (LH ascending above RH) is notated and intended
- Incomplete chords: the missing 3rd or 5th of a chord is acceptable when it would compromise a
  voice's smooth motion
- Parallel octaves between outer voices and parallel fifths are both forbidden

---

### Fugue Structure

1. **Exposition:** Each voice enters in turn with the subject, alternating tonic and dominant (real
   or tonal answer)
2. **Countersubject:** A consistent countermelody accompanying the subject in subsequent entries;
   must be invertible
3. **Episodes:** Sequential passages using subject fragments; modulatory
4. **Middle entries:** Subject reappears in various key areas
5. **Stretto:** Subject entries overlap, each beginning before the previous completes
6. **Final entry:** In tonic, often preceded by a dominant pedal point

## 2. Classical Era (approx. 1750–1820)

**Key composers:** Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, Clementi

### Core Concept: Melody + Accompaniment

The Classical era codified a two-layer texture: a singing right-hand melody over a steady left-hand
accompaniment. Both hands still require skill, but their roles are clearly differentiated.

---

### The Alberti Bass

The defining left-hand texture. Named for Domenico Alberti (c. 1710–1740).

**Pattern:** Low note → high note → middle note → high note (repeating)

For a C major chord in 4/4 with eighth notes: **C–G–E–G–C–G–E–G**

- **Which hand:** Always the left hand
- **Register:** Centered below middle C; bass note well below, chord tones in the middle register
- **When used:** Under sustained, singing right-hand melodies. Not in development sections, not in
  loud chordal passages, not in contrapuntal passages
- **Function:** Provides harmonic clarity and rhythmic momentum without drawing attention to itself.
  The lowest note of each group defines the chord. **Typical range for the full pattern:** Bass note
  around C3 or lower; chord tones up to around C4.

---

### Singing Melodic Lines (Right Hand)

**Cantabile** (singing) melody is the Classical ideal:

- Notes within a phrase played **legato** (slurred) — a new requirement relative to Baroque
  detachment
- Dynamic shape through the phrase: begin moderately, swell toward climax, taper at cadence
- Expressive notes (appoggiaturas, chromatic neighbors) receive subtle emphasis
- Final phrase note receives a brief lightening — the "taper" **Right-hand texture:** Melody in the
  topmost voice, inner voices played more softly, creating layered balance within one hand.

---

### Phrase Structure: Antecedent/Consequent

The Classical era codified **periodic phrase structure**:

- **Antecedent (4 bars):** Ends on half cadence (V) or imperfect authentic cadence — harmonically
  open
- **Consequent (4 bars):** Begins similarly; ends on perfect authentic cadence (I) — closed
  Together: an **8-bar period**.

**Extensions:**

- Cadential extension: repeating/elaborating the final cadence (2–4 extra bars)
- Phrase expansion: inserting extra material mid-phrase (Beethoven specialty)
- Elision: last beat of one phrase = first beat of the next

---

### Sonata Form: Pianistic Textures by Section

| Section                   | Typical Piano Texture                                             |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Exposition: First theme   | Alberti bass, cantabile melody, home tonic feel                   |
| Exposition: Bridge        | Scales, arpeggios, passage work; modulates to dominant            |
| Exposition: Second theme  | Contrasting character; new key area                               |
| Exposition: Closing theme | Scales converging on cadences; passage work                       |
| Development               | Alberti disappears; fragmented motives, remote keys, counterpoint |
| Recapitulation            | Returns to first-theme texture; bridge modified to stay in tonic  |
| Coda                      | Brief (Haydn/Mozart) or extended (Beethoven)                      |

**Key gesture:** Both hands converge from opposite extremes in parallel scales or arpeggios, landing
together on a cadential chord.

---

### Classical Ornaments

| Ornament     | Classical Practice                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Appoggiatura | Played on the beat; takes half the main note's value (or 2/3 for dotted main notes). Long and metrically significant.                                                                                                     |
| Trill        | May start on main note (not just upper auxiliary as in Baroque). Context-dependent. Cadential trills tend to start on upper note; mid-phrase trills sometimes start on written note. Ends with a turn unless impractical. |
| Turn         | Four-note figure: upper → main → lower → main. Placed on note = begins immediately; placed after note = fills the space between two notes.                                                                                |
| Mordent      | Less common than in Baroque. Lower mordent (main → lower → main, very fast) on strong beats for rhythmic emphasis.                                                                                                        |

---

### Dynamic Markings

- **p / f:** Foundational contrast; sudden shifts are meaningful and structural
- **pp / ff:** Expanded range (especially Beethoven)
- **< >** (hairpins): Gradual changes within phrases — used sparingly by Haydn/Mozart, dramatically
  by Beethoven
- **sf / sfz (sforzando):** Sudden sharp accent on a single note or chord, at any dynamic level.
  Beethoven's signature. On unexpected beats creates maximum disruption.
- **fp (forte-piano):** Attack forte, immediately drop to piano. A "punch-and-release" effect.

---

### Beethoven's Innovations

**Extended dynamic range:** ppp through fff; sudden dramatic reversals (fff → pp in a single
measure).

**Sforzando on unexpected beats:** Sforzandos placed on beat 2 in 3/4, or on weak eighth notes —
creating rhythmic disruption against regular metric expectations.

**Motivic development:** Entire movements built from a single short motive. The development section
systematically fragments, inverts, and transposes the motive through remote keys.

**Extreme register use:** Very high treble and very deep bass, thunderous low octaves. Pushes the
6-octave fortepiano to its limits.

**Contrapuntal integration:** Late Beethoven (Op. 101, 106, 109, 110, 111) incorporates full fugues,
canons, and inversional counterpoint into the sonata framework.

## 3. Romantic Era (approx. 1820–1900)

**Key composers:** Chopin, Liszt, Schumann, Brahms, Schubert

### Chopin: Nocturne Texture

**Left hand:** Bass note on beat 1 (low register, often a single note or octave), followed by a
mid-register chord on beats 2 and 3. The pedal blends them into a harmonic wash. Left hand spans
large intervals — tenths are common.

**Right hand:** Long, singing melody with elaborate fioritura (ornamental flourishes) at cadences —
cascading chromatic runs and arpeggios notated with small notes.

**Rubato:** The left hand keeps strict time; the right hand breathes, rushes, or lingers
independently. The melody fluctuates; the accompaniment is the clock.

**Pedaling:** Hold through the bass note and chord, blending harmonically. Chopin's pedal markings
are precise and detailed. Some passages use intentional blur (pedal held across harmonic changes).

**Key examples:** Op. 9 No. 2 (E♭ major), Op. 27 No. 2 (D♭ major), Op. 48 No. 1 (C minor)

---

### Chopin: Mazurka Rhythm

- **Meter:** 3/4
- **Defining feature:** Accent displacement to **beat 2 or beat 3** (not beat 1)
- **Characteristic figure:** Dotted quarter on beat 2 (accented) → eighth on beat 3
- **Folk influences:** Lydian raised 4th, modal harmonies, drone fifths in the bass (imitating
  Polish folk instruments)

### Chopin: Polonaise Rhythm

- **Meter:** 3/4
- **Characteristic figure:** Eighth note + two sixteenths at the opening of each bar
- **Character:** Stately, martial, ceremonial
- **Bass:** Steady quarter-note or half-note pulse
- **Op. 53 "Heroic" example:** 16-bar repeated bass octaves on A (pedal tone) under right-hand
  melody

---

### Chopin: Études as Technical Studies

Each étude isolates a specific technical challenge:

| Étude                         | Technical Focus                                                              |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Op. 10 No. 1                  | Wide broken-chord arpeggios; hand stretch/expansion                          |
| Op. 10 No. 2                  | Chromatic melody in fingers 3-4-5 while 1-2 hold chord — finger independence |
| Op. 10 No. 12 "Revolutionary" | Left-hand chromatic runs across the full keyboard                            |
| Op. 25 No. 6                  | Parallel chromatic thirds in both hands                                      |
| Op. 25 No. 11 "Winter Wind"   | Fast chromatic figurations + octave melody                                   |
| Op. 10 No. 5                  | Right hand on black keys only; different hand posture                        |

---

### Liszt: Octave Technique

Playing melodic lines in parallel octaves — both notes struck simultaneously. Requires:

- Wrist rotation rather than arm pounding to avoid fatigue
- Even speed and equal weight on both notes
- Extended passages (16–32 bars) at forte or fortissimo **Transcription technique:** Representing
  orchestral forces on solo piano:

| Orchestral texture    | Piano realization                                                  |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Singing strings       | Cantabile RH, sustained pedal                                      |
| Sustained wind chords | Held middle-register chords, both hands                            |
| Pizzicato bass        | Short, detached bass notes, no pedal                               |
| Brass fanfares        | ff octave doublings, both hands                                    |
| Timpani rolls         | Rapid tremolo bass octaves (low notes alternated quickly)          |
| Full climax           | Both hands in full-texture ff chords, wide register, maximum pedal |

**Tremolo technique:** Rapid alternation of the same pitch between low and middle octave, simulating
sustained orchestral string tremolo.

**Hungarian Minor scale:** 1–2–♭3–♯4–5–♭6–7 (two augmented seconds). Creates exotic, melismatic
melodic lines with wide leaps and ornamental chromatic turns around the augmented-second steps.

---

### Schumann: Inner Voices, Polyrhythm, Displacement

**Inner voices:** Melodic lines in the middle register (not the top or bass). The "melody" is
sometimes in the thumb of the right hand — physically less natural to emphasize, requiring
deliberate balance. (_Kinderscenen_, _Kreisleriana_)

**Rhythmic displacement:** Consistent groups of three eighth notes in a 4/4 measure create displaced
accents that conflict with the barline — the implied accent shifts every three eighth notes. Notated
normally; the phrasing implies the displacement. (_Toccata_ Op. 7)

**Polyrhythm (2 against 3):** One hand plays triplets, the other plays duplets. Both must maintain
their respective rhythms without adjusting. (_Carnaval_, _Davidsbündlertänze_)

**Syncopation:** Precisely marked accent marks (>) on non-downbeat positions.

---

### Brahms: Thick Textures, Cross-Rhythms, 3rds and 6ths

**Thick chordal textures:** Chords of 4–5 notes in each hand, spread to tenths or more. Each voice
within the chord has independent melodic motion.

**Cross-rhythms:** While one hand plays in groups of 3, the other plays in groups of 2 (or 4). In
_Intermezzo_ Op. 118 No. 2: RH melody moves in implied three-note cells, LH accompaniment in
two-note cells — both fitting within the notated 3/4. Neither is "wrong"; both are simultaneous
metric layers.

**Intervals of 3rds and 6ths:** Right-hand melodies doubled a third or sixth below the main voice.
The challenge: equal weight on both notes simultaneously. Brahms uses parallel 3rds and 6ths at
greater length and saturation than any predecessor.

**Left-hand writing:** Often contains two independent lines — a bass note and an inner melodic
voice. Both are genuinely melodic; neither is subordinate accompaniment.

---

### Schubert: Song Accompaniment Patterns

Schubert's piano textures derive directly from his Lieder accompaniments:

**Persistent ostinato pattern:** A single accompaniment figure maintained through an entire piece or
large section, creating atmosphere through persistence. (_Erlkönig_: continuous triplet octaves
throughout)

**Arpeggiated bass variants:**

- Bass note held with pedal, chord tones rolled upward above it
- Slow arpeggiation of each chord (harp-like)
- Sustained bass + repeating chords above in a ticking pattern **"Floating" accompaniment:** (_Auf
  dem Wasser zu singen_): Broken triplet figure rising and falling within each chord, creating a
  sense of suspension.

**Long stasis + sudden chromatic shift:** Maintains a simple accompaniment pattern for many bars
while harmony barely moves — then a sudden third-relation shift (e.g., C major → E♭ major directly)
disrupts the stasis. The pianist must sustain even, undifferentiated accompaniment for extended
periods.

---

### Sustain Pedal in Romantic Music

The pedal becomes a primary compositional element, not merely a convenience.

| Pedal Technique             | Usage                                                                               |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Harmonic pedal              | Changed with each harmony, on the beat — sustain within each chord                  |
| Syncopated pedal            | Changed slightly after the beat: captures new bass without blurring two harmonies   |
| Long pedal across harmonies | Intentional blur — Chopin uses this in the Fantasie-Impromptu and certain nocturnes |
| Half pedal                  | Partial release clears some harmonics; used for gradual clearing                    |

---

### Extended Tonality and Chromaticism

**Chromatic voice leading:** Individual voices move by half steps between chords. (_Chopin Prelude
Op. 28 No. 4_: chromatic left-hand descent through all 12 pitches over 12 bars)

**Third relationships (Chromatic mediants):** Direct motion between keys or chords a major or minor
third apart, without passing through the dominant. One voice moves by half step; two notes are
common tones. Schubert uses these as structural pivots.

**Enharmonic modulation:** Reinterpreting a diminished 7th chord (which has four possible
leading-tone readings) to pivot to a distant key. Spelling changes (C# → D♭) to reflect the new
tonal context.

**Altered dominants:** V7 with raised or lowered 5th. Creates a more poignant approach to the tonic.
Common in Chopin nocturnes before the final resolution.

## Cross-Era Comparison

| Feature           | Baroque                                     | Classical                                    | Romantic                                             |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Left hand role    | Independent melodic voice                   | Alberti bass / harmonic support              | Arpeggiated, inner voices, octave patterns           |
| Melody location   | Any voice (polyphonic)                      | Right hand, top voice only                   | Right hand, highly ornamented, cantabile             |
| Ornamentation     | Structural, begins on beat, upper auxiliary | Functional, notated, context-dependent start | Expressive, often written out, rhythmically flexible |
| Dynamics          | Terraced (sudden shifts)                    | Contrasting + hairpins                       | Continuous shaping, ppp–fff range                    |
| Harmonic rhythm   | Steady, bass-defined                        | Moderate, Alberti-defined                    | Slow + sudden remote shifts                          |
| Pedal             | None (harpsichord) / sparse                 | Occasional color                             | Integral to texture; defined in score                |
| Phrase structure  | Continuous spinning-out                     | Regular 4+4 periods                          | Extended, asymmetric, through-composed               |
| Texture archetype | Contrapuntal (equal voices)                 | Melody + accompaniment                       | Multi-layered, orchestral, thick                     |
