# Impressionism, Modernism, Minimalism, and Contemporary Art Music

---

## 1. Impressionism (approx. 1890–1930)

**Key composers:** Claude Debussy, Maurice Ravel, Erik Satie

### Core Concept: Sound as Color

Impressionist piano writing treats chords and scales as **timbral objects** rather than functional
harmonic agents. The question is not "where does this chord lead?" but "what color does this chord
produce?" Traditional voice-leading rules are intentionally suspended in favor of registral blend
and sensory effect.

---

### Scale Sources

**Whole-tone scale:** All six pitches separated by whole steps. Only two transpositions exist
(C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A♯ and D♭–E♭–F–G–A–B).

- Every triad built on the scale is augmented — no tonal pull exists
- Motion between chords feels suspended or floating
- No leading tone → no dominant-to-tonic resolution
- **Debussy's use:** "Voiles" (Préludes Book I, No. 2) is almost entirely whole-tone; "Pagodes" uses
  it for an exotic, static quality **Pentatonic scale (major):** C–D–E–G–A (no 4th or 7th). Open,
  non-dissonant quality from the absence of semitones.

- Used for pastoral, archaic, or folk-like character
- "La fille aux cheveux de lin" (Préludes I, No. 8): nearly exclusively pentatonic melody
- "La cathédrale engloutie" (Préludes I, No. 10): pentatonic mixed with parallel fifths for an
  archaic bell-like sound **Modes:** | Mode | Key characteristic | Impressionist use | |---|---|---|
  | Dorian | Natural minor with raised 6th | Folk/archaic quality ("La soirée dans Grenade") | |
  Lydian | Major with raised 4th | Bright, hovering, unresolved (Satie Gymnopédies) | | Mixolydian |
  Major with lowered 7th | Avoiding the leading tone for modal color | | Phrygian | Lowered 2nd |
  Spanish-inflected pieces ("La Puerta del Vino") |

---

### Parallel Motion (Planing)

One of Impressionism's most recognizable departures from common-practice voice leading.

**Parallel fifths and octaves:** Used deliberately for an archaic, organum-like or gamelan-like
sound. NOT an error.

- "La cathédrale engloutie": massive parallel fifth/octave stacks moving in parallel — suggests
  great bells **Diatonic planing:** A chord shape moves in parallel motion, but each chord's quality
  adjusts to remain diatonic to the key (quality changes as you move through the scale).

**Chromatic planing:** A chord shape moves in exact parallel motion, all voices moving by the same
interval, quality preserved. More dissonant; used by Debussy.

**Parallel 9th chord streams (Ravel):** Both hands moving the same 9th chord shape in parallel,
chromatically or diatonically. Creates a dense, shimmering wall of sound.

---

### Left Hand Patterns

**Pedal tone (sustained bass):** A single low pitch held (or rearticulated) for many bars while
upper voices change. Creates harmonic ambiguity — the harmony "floats" above an anchored bass.
Common in Debussy's seascape pieces.

**Low bass clusters/rumbles:** Left hand in the contra-octave register (below C2). Wide-spaced
perfect fifths or open octaves, occasionally with added chromatic neighbors. Held with pedal to
produce a resonant blur. Debussy uses this for large spaces, water, storms.

**Bell-like patterns (high register):** Very soft isolated pitches or sparse intervals in the
highest octave, with long vibration tails via pedal. The LH may be completely absent during these
passages.

---

### Pedal Technique

The sustain pedal becomes a compositional element:

- **Long pedal with intentional blur:** Pedal held through harmonic changes. Creates a wash of
  harmonics. Debussy notates "laissez vibrer" (let ring).
- **Half-pedal:** Partially released to clear bass while retaining upper harmonics. Requires
  specific technique but produces gradual clearing.
- **Una corda:** Used for timbre change (thin, glassy sound), not just dynamic reduction.
- **Notation:** Debussy's scores have precise pedal markings, including pedal held across apparent
  clashes. Wavy pedal lines indicate variable release.

---

### Ravel vs. Debussy vs. Satie

| Feature              | Debussy                                             | Ravel                                                        | Satie                                 |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| Articulation         | Fluid, blurred                                      | Precise, clear                                               | Dry, even, unexpressive               |
| Tempo markings       | Poetic ("aussi lentement que possible")             | Exact metronomic                                             | Fixed, non-rubato                     |
| Pedal                | Extensive blurring                                  | Clean arpeggiation written out                               | Minimal                               |
| Rhythm               | Complex tuplets, metric ambiguity                   | Regular rhythmic units even in dense texture                 | Metronomic, non-expressive            |
| Harmony              | Truly non-functional                                | Often functionally recoverable (secondary dominants present) | Static oscillation between two chords |
| Dynamics             | Extreme pppp                                        | Carefully graded                                             | Consistently soft, no climaxes        |
| AI notation accuracy | Approximate — requires performance beyond the score | Accurate — fully notatable                                   | Highly accurate                       |

**Satie "Gymnopédies" texture (1888):**

- LH: Quarter note (bass) + two quarter notes (chord) in 3/4 — beat 1 is a bass note, beats 2–3 are
  a chord voicing. This pattern repeats throughout without variation.
- The chord on beats 2–3 is typically a 9th chord (major 7th + 9th, no 5th) in the middle register.
- Bass note: perfect 5th or open interval.
- RH: Simple, stepwise melody in middle register. Quarter and half notes only. Zero ornamentation.
- Character: Consistently soft, no crescendos of consequence, no climaxes. Metronomic.
- Harmonic language: Diatonic with Lydian inflection. "No. 1" oscillates between D major 9th and G
  major 9th — an unresolved dominant quality that never forcefully resolves.

---

### Characteristic Rhythms and Meters

- 3/4 and 9/8 predominate; binary meters less characteristic
- Cross-rhythms: 3-against-2, 3-against-4 (especially Ravel)
- Metric ambiguity: barlines often don't coincide with melodic accent
- Habanera rhythm (dotted-eighth + sixteenth + two eighths) in Spanish-influenced pieces
- Irregular phrase lengths: 5-bar, 7-bar phrases rather than symmetric 4-bar structures

## 2. Neo-Classicism and Modernism (1920–1960)

### Prokofiev: Motoric Rhythms and Percussive Attack

**Core concept:** The piano as a percussion instrument with pitch.

**Motoric rhythm:** Constant, machine-like subdivision — eighth or sixteenth notes at fast tempo.
The rhythm does not breathe. Downbeats are often harshly accented.

**Left hand:** Eighth-note chord stabs or octave bass notes on every beat. Often doubles the
right-hand rhythm rather than providing contrasting accompaniment.

**Right hand:** Rapid repeated-note figures, staccato articulation, often in octaves or
close-position chords. Forte or fortissimo as default in fast passages.

**Dissonant counterpoint:** Two independent voices moving simultaneously, each with its own logic,
creating clashing intervals (major 7ths, minor 9ths, tritones). Dissonances are controlled but
resolution is deferred.

**Harmonic language:** Tonal but "spiky" — key centers maintained, but chromatic neighbor tones,
raised/lowered scale degrees, and modal shifts create "wrong note" dissonances. Always recoverable
to a tonal center, but the surface is harsh.

**Key works:** "War Sonatas" (Nos. 6, 7, 8), Sarcasms Op. 17, Visions fugitives Op. 22.

---

### Bartók: Folk-Derived Scales

**Acoustic scale (Lydian-Mixolydian / Overtone Scale):** C–D–E–F♯–G–A–B♭ — contains both the raised
4th of Lydian and the lowered 7th of Mixolydian. Approximates the natural overtone series. The most
characteristic Bartók sonority.

**Octatonic scale:** Alternating half and whole steps (two transpositions: C–C♯–D♯–E–F♯–G–A–B♭ and
C–D–E♭–F–F♯–G♯–A–B). Produces dense, ambiguous tonality; contains multiple tritones and
diminished-seventh subsets.

**Hungarian/Eastern European folk modes:** Phrygian, Dorian, Lydian — absorbed from Bartók's
transcription of thousands of folk melodies.

**Hands in 2nds and minor 9ths:** Both hands placed a major 2nd or minor 9th apart, moving in
parallel or contrary motion. Intensely dissonant but used as a structural interval. (_Mikrokosmos_
Vol. IV, No. 132 "Seconds")

**Night Music texture:** A slow, atmospheric texture evoking nocturnal outdoor scenes:

- Sparse, isolated high-register pitches (chromatic or quartal) in the RH, widely spaced in time,
  with silences between
- Slow-moving, sustained bass in the LH — pedal tone or slowly shifting harmony
- Chromatic "insect" figures: rapid chromatic trills or ornaments as grace note groups
- Very soft dynamics with sudden brief sforzandi
- Arhythmic upper voice notated with complex values (32nds, triplets) to create unmetered effect
  within a metrical grid **Key work:** "Out of Doors" Suite No. 4 "The Night's Music" (1926)

**Bulgarian rhythms and asymmetric meters:**

| Meter | Grouping                            |
| ----- | ----------------------------------- |
| 5/8   | 2+3 or 3+2                          |
| 7/8   | 2+2+3, 3+2+2, or 2+3+2              |
| 8/8   | 3+2+3                               |
| 9/8   | 2+2+2+3 or other additive groupings |

Notation: Bartók marks beat groupings with beaming and accent marks. Both hands share the same
asymmetric grouping.

**Key work:** _Mikrokosmos_ Vol. VI, Nos. 148–153 "Six Dances in Bulgarian Rhythm"

**Percussive use of the piano:** Staccato and non-legato as default; heavy forearm weight; no pedal
(or minimal); sforzandi on weak beats; cluster chords (fist, forearm, palm).

---

### Stravinsky: Bitonality, Ostinato, Displacement

**Bitonality:** Two key centers sounding simultaneously, each in a different register or hand. The
"Petrushka chord": C major + F♯ major (tritone apart) superimposed.

**For the piano:** LH establishes one key (root position or open-fifth vamp), RH plays in a
contrasting key (semitone or tritone away). Both hands must be internally consonant; dissonance is
between layers, not within them.

**Ostinato:** Relentlessly repeated rhythmic/melodic cell. Not development — repetition as
structure. Typically in the LH; RH varies above it.

**Rhythmic displacement:** A melodic/rhythmic pattern begins on a different beat in successive
repetitions without changing the notated meter. The perceived downbeat shifts, creating metric
displacement without a time signature change.

**Constant meter change:** Time signature changes every bar or few beats (3/8, 2/8, 5/16, 4/8 in a
single passage). Different from Bartók's consistent asymmetric meters.

**Key works:** "Piano Rag-Music" (1919), "Serenade in A" (1925), "Sonata for Piano" (1924).

## 3. Minimalism (1960s–present)

**Key composers:** Philip Glass, Steve Reich, Arvo Pärt, Ludovico Einaudi

### Philip Glass: Cycling Arpeggios and Additive Process

**Basic texture:** Continuous arpeggiated figure in eighth or sixteenth notes, cycling through the
pitches of a chord or short melodic cell without interruption. Both hands often play the same figure
in different octaves.

**Additive melody:** A figure begins with a small number of notes and gains notes at the beginning
or end in each repetition:

```
[E–A–B] → [D–E–A–B] → [C–D–E–A–B] → [B–C–D–E–A–B]
```

**Harmonic rhythm:** Extremely slow. A single harmony persists for 4, 8, 16 or more bars. When
harmony changes, it typically moves by diatonic step or third — not by dominant function.

**Rhythmic character:** Absolutely metronomic. No rubato, no accent variation. The barline organizes
the pattern without creating metric accent.

**Key works:** "Metamorphosis 1–5" (1988), "Glassworks" (1981), "Mad Rush" (1979)

---

### Steve Reich: Phasing

**Phasing technique:** Two performers (or voices) play the same repeating pattern at slightly
different tempos until one is displaced by one sixteenth note, then one eighth note, etc. All
possible phase relationships are cycled through before returning to unison.

**"Piano Phase" (1967):** Two pianos play the same 12-note melodic cell
(E–F♯–B–C♯–D–F♯–E–C♯–B–F♯–D–C♯) in rapid continuous eighth notes. One pianist gradually speeds up
while the other holds tempo.

**What is heard:** As the phase shifts, implied melodies and rhythms emerge from the combination
that exist in neither part alone.

**Notation:** Standard notation with instruction to gradually accelerate one part. The fully phased
result would be extremely complex to notate exactly.

---

### Arvo Pärt: Tintinnabuli Method

Pärt's tintinnabuli technique uses exactly two voices:

**T-voice (tintinnabuli = "little bells"):**

- Contains only the notes of the tonic triad (scale degrees 1, 3, 5)
- Never departs from the tonic triad
- Moves according to position rules relative to the M-voice **M-voice (melodic voice):**
- Moves stepwise through a mode or diatonic scale
- Contains all scale degrees **Position rules for the T-voice:** Pärt specifies 1st position
  (nearest triad tone above the M-voice note), 2nd position (second nearest above), or below
  variants.

**Example (A minor, M-voice ascending, T-voice 1st position above):**

| M-voice | T-voice (nearest triad tone above) |
| ------- | ---------------------------------- |
| A       | C                                  |
| B       | E                                  |
| C       | E                                  |
| D       | E                                  |
| E       | A                                  |
| F       | A                                  |
| G       | A                                  |
| A       | C (octave up)                      |

**Result:** A two-voice combination that is tonally stable but not harmonically directed. Simple in
construction, deeply meditative in effect.

**Rhythm:** Free — no prescribed tempo or strict meter in some works. "Für Alina" (1976) is
completely free rhythmically.

**Key works:** "Für Alina" (1976), "Spiegel im Spiegel" (1978, violin/piano), "Variationen zur
Gesundung von Arinuschka" (1977)

---

### Ludovico Einaudi: Neo-Romantic Ostinato

**Left hand patterns (one of these is always present):**

1. Sustained bass note (root, held with pedal through multiple beats) + mid-register chord on weak
   beats
2. Arpeggiated pattern: constant 8th or 16th subdivision through chord tones
3. Drone bass: one low pitch sustained through many bars while harmony shifts above it **Right
   hand:** Stepwise, singable melody in middle-to-upper register — pentatonic or simple diatonic.
   Sometimes the RH also plays ostinato arpeggios in the middle register while a higher voice
   carries the melody.

**Harmonic language:** Simple diatonic progressions (I–V–vi–IV), largely triadic with added 9ths or
occasional modal color. Emotional and textural interest, not harmonic innovation.

**Accumulation:** Pieces begin sparse, gradually add layers, increase dynamics, intensify the
ostinato. Arc is emotional and dynamic rather than structural/process-based.

**Key works:** "Nuvole Bianche," "Experience," "Primavera," "Le Onde"

## 4. Contemporary Art Music (1950–present)

### Twelve-Tone / Serial Technique

A 12-note row (series) is the source of all pitch material. The row can appear in four forms:

- **Prime (P):** Original order
- **Retrograde (R):** Reversed
- **Inversion (I):** Intervals inverted
- **Retrograde Inversion (RI):** Both reversed and inverted Each of the 4 forms can begin on any of
  12 pitches = 48 possible row forms. **Webern's approach:** Ultra-sparse. Single notes, not chords.
  Extreme dynamic contrasts. Wide registral leaps. Row broken into trichords (3-note cells) that
  return in different transpositions. _Variations for Piano_ Op. 27 (1936).

**Schoenberg's approach:** Denser, more Romantic in texture. Dance forms (Gavotte, Minuet, Gigue)
with 12-tone technique applied within. _Suite for Piano_ Op. 25 (1923) — the first completely
12-tone piano work.

**Integral serialism (Boulez, Stockhausen, Babbitt):** Extends serial technique to dynamics,
articulation, duration, and register — every parameter is serialized. Boulez's _Piano Sonata No. 2_
(1948): notation so complex it approaches the limits of readability.

---

### Messiaen: Modes of Limited Transposition

Modes of limited transposition return to themselves after fewer than 12 transpositions due to
internal symmetry. The most important:

| Mode               | Structure                   | Character                             |
| ------------------ | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Mode 1             | Whole-tone scale            | 2 transpositions — floating, static   |
| Mode 2 (Octatonic) | Half-whole alternating      | 3 transpositions — dense, ambiguous   |
| Mode 3             | Tone–semitone–semitone (×3) | 4 transpositions — complex, chromatic |

**Color-harmony associations:** Messiaen had synesthetic associations between chords/modes and
colors. Compositional choices were driven by sonic-color combinations, not traditional resolution.

**Bird song transcription:** Messiaen transcribed actual bird calls:

- Transposed down into the piano's range (birds sing in extreme upper registers)
- Notated in approximate rhythm (very short note values: 64th notes, complex tuplets)
- Placed in high piano register
- Rapid ornaments approximate the bird's articulation **Rhythmic techniques:**
- **Non-retrogradable rhythms:** Palindromic rhythms reading the same forward and backward
- **Added values:** Inserting a short duration into a regular pattern to disrupt it
- **Hindu tala influence:** Augmentation and diminution applied to entire rhythmic cells **Key
  works:** "Catalogue d'oiseaux" (1958), "Vingt regards sur l'Enfant-Jésus" (1944)

---

### Ligeti: Étude Patterns and Polyrhythmic Complexity

**Étude No. 1 "Désordre":**

- RH: white keys only; grouped as 3+5=8 eighth notes per implied bar
- LH: black keys only; grouped as 4+4=8 eighth notes
- Both begin at same tempo; LH drops notes periodically, creating gradual desynchronization
- Result: gradually shifting polytonal, polyrhythmic texture from two simple patterns **Étude No. 4
  "Fanfares":**
- LH: rapid 3+3+2 ostinato pattern
- RH: fanfare-like melodic cells against it at irregular intervals
- The two never align rhythmically except by coincidence **Étude No. 6 "Automne à Varsovie":**
- Falling chromatic lines in multiple voices, each at different rates — mechanical decay image
  **Notation:** So complex that standard notation often requires multiple simultaneous tempo
  markings, mixed subdivisions (quintuplets against septuplets), rehearsal letters at every bar.

---

### Extended Techniques

**Prepared piano (John Cage):** Objects (bolts, screws, rubber erasers, felt) placed on or between
piano strings. The same key produces an altered timbre — percussive, muted, buzzing, or metallic.

**Notation:** A "preparation table" at the beginning of the score specifies object placement.
Standard pitch notation is then used — the transformation is physical.

**Key work:** Cage, "Sonatas and Interludes" (1948) — prepared piano resembling a gamelan ensemble.

**Cluster chords:**

| Type            | Performance                                   |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Fist cluster    | Vertical fist pressing multiple adjacent keys |
| Forearm cluster | Forearm laid across keys for wide spans       |
| Palm cluster    | Palm pressed in various orientations          |

**Notation conventions for clusters:**

- Cowell/Bartók: Bracket spanning the cluster range with pitches at top and bottom, indicating all
  keys between are pressed
- Filled rectangular note head for black-key clusters; open for white-key clusters

## Cross-Style Summary

| Feature            | Impressionism                        | Neo-Classicism                       | Minimalism                    | Contemporary                   |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Primary scales     | Whole-tone, pentatonic, modes        | Folk, acoustic, octatonic            | Diatonic modes, triads        | 12-tone rows, Messiaen modes   |
| Rhythmic character | Fluid, flexible, asymmetric          | Motoric/percussive or asymmetric     | Metronomic, additive          | Complex polyrhythm, serialized |
| Harmonic motion    | Static, coloristic                   | Dissonant but directed               | Very slow or modal stasis     | Non-functional, systematic     |
| Pedal use          | Extensive, blurring                  | Minimal (Bartók), moderate           | Moderate, sustain for texture | Specified precisely or avoided |
| Texture archetype  | Parallel chord streams, arpeggiation | Motoric octaves, contrapuntal voices | Cycling arpeggios, ostinato   | Clusters, polyrhythm, extremes |
| LH role            | Pedal tones, bass rumbles            | Rhythmic equal or counterpoint       | Pattern, drone, ostinato      | Equal or contrasting layer     |
| Key composers      | Debussy, Ravel, Satie                | Prokofiev, Bartók, Stravinsky        | Glass, Reich, Pärt, Einaudi   | Messiaen, Ligeti, Schoenberg   |
