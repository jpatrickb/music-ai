# Ragtime, Blues, Stride, and Boogie-Woogie

---

## 1. Ragtime (approx. 1895–1920)

**Key composers:** Scott Joplin, James Scott, Joseph Lamb

### Core Concept: "Ragged" Syncopation over a March Beat

Ragtime combines a steady, march-like left-hand pulse with a persistently syncopated right-hand
melody. The term "ragged time" refers to the right hand's habit of anticipating downbeats and
displacing accents.

---

### Meter and Form

**Meter:** 2/4 or cut time (march-like). Joplin explicitly instructs "Do not play this piece fast.
It is never right to play Ragtime fast." (_Maple Leaf Rag_ and others).

**Standard form — 16-bar strains:**

| Form        | Layout                            |
| ----------- | --------------------------------- |
| Simple      | AABB                              |
| Full        | AABBCCDD (as in _Maple Leaf Rag_) |
| With return | AABBACCAA or similar              |

Each strain is 16 bars, repeated before moving to the next. A 4-bar introduction typically precedes
the first strain.

---

### Left Hand: The "Oom-Pah" Pattern

In 2/4 time (four beats felt as: 1 – and – 2 – and):

| Beat   | What to play                                 | Typical pitch                                       |
| ------ | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Beat 1 | Single bass note                             | Root of chord, low register                         |
| Beat 2 | Mid-register chord (3 notes)                 | Triad or dom. 7th, no bass doubling                 |
| Beat 3 | Single bass note                             | 5th of chord, or root an octave up, or passing tone |
| Beat 4 | Mid-register chord (same or slightly varied) | Same as beat 2                                      |

**"Oom"** = bass notes (beats 1 and 3); **"pah"** = chords (beats 2 and 4).

For a C major chord: C (beat 1) / E–G–C chord (beat 2) / G (beat 3) / E–G–C chord (beat 4).

**Register:** Bass notes in the octave below middle C (around C3–G2); chord voicings from about
C3–C4.

---

### Right Hand: Syncopation

**The "ragged" concept:** The right hand anticipates downbeats by one eighth note. A note falls on
the "and of 4" of one bar, tied over to beat 1 of the next — the downbeat is implied but not
sounded.

**Common patterns (2/4, eighth-note level):**

1. **Tied syncopation:** Eighth note on the "and of 1" tied to beat 2 — creates rhythmic
   displacement
2. **Three-against-two feel:** Groups of three eighth notes cutting across the two-beat measure
3. **Dotted-eighth/sixteenth figures:** Landing just before a beat, then on an offbeat — the
   characteristic "lurch" forward In notation: dotted rhythms, eighth-note syncopations, and tied
   notes across barlines, all against the mechanically even left-hand pulse.

---

### Harmonic Vocabulary

- **V7 chords** (with 7th resolving downward) — prominent throughout
- **Secondary dominants:** V7/IV, V7/V, V7/II common in chain progressions
- **Diminished 7th chords** as passing/embellishing chords in transitional bars
- **Modulation to the subdominant (IV) for the Trio** — the single most important harmonic feature
  of classic ragtime. A piece in C moves to F; a piece in G moves to C.

---

### The Trio Section

The Trio is typically the third strain (C section):

- **Key:** Always the subdominant (IV) — warmer, more lyrical
- **Character:** More song-like, less harmonically active than earlier strains
- **Origin:** Borrowed directly from march form

---

## 2. Stride Piano (1920s–1940s)

**Key figures:** James P. Johnson, Fats Waller, Willie "The Lion" Smith, Art Tatum

### Core Concept: The Piano as a Full Band

Stride piano emerged from ragtime and set out to replace an entire dance band with ten fingers. The
explicit four-layer texture:

1. **Bass line (bass/tuba):** LH, beats 1 and 3 — single notes in the low register
2. **Chord stabs (brass/rhythm):** LH, beats 2 and 4 — mid-range chord voicings
3. **Inner accompaniment:** RH inner voices or fills between melody notes
4. **Melody:** RH, upper register

---

### Left Hand: The Full Stride Pattern

**The key innovation over ragtime:** The register gap between bass notes and chord voicings
dramatically expands — typically a 10th to two octaves in a single beat.

| Beat   | What to play                            | Register                        |
| ------ | --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Beat 1 | Single bass note (or 10th)              | Lowest octave of piano          |
| Beat 2 | Full chord voicing (7th or 9th chord)   | Middle register, centered on C4 |
| Beat 3 | Bass note (or 5th / chromatic approach) | Lowest octave                   |
| Beat 4 | Full chord voicing                      | Middle register                 |

The physical jump on each half-beat — from low bass to mid-range chord — covers a **minor 7th to
major 10th** in a fraction of a second, at tempos that can exceed 200 BPM.

**Standard chord voicings on beats 2 and 4:**

- C6: C–E–G–A (major 6th — common tonic substitute)
- C7: C–E–G–B♭
- Dm7: D–F–A–C

---

### The "Tenth" Bass

A James P. Johnson / Fats Waller signature: instead of a single root note on beats 1 and 3, play a
**10th interval** simultaneously:

- Root in the lowest bass register
- 3rd of the chord an octave above the root's octave For a C chord: C (two octaves below middle C) +
  E (just below middle C) played together. This implies the chord quality without a full voicing.
  Requires a large hand span or a slightly rolled interval.

---

### Right Hand: Melody + Fills

- **Chord-melody:** Melody harmonized in the top voice of RH chords (not single-note melody)
- **Scalar runs:** Chromatic/scalar lines fill space between melody notes
- **Octave melody:** Melody doubled in octaves for power and volume
- **Contrary-motion fills:** RH moves against the direction implied by the LH bass

---

### Harmonic Vocabulary

Far more sophisticated than ragtime:

- **Extended chords standard:** Major 7ths, dominant 9ths, dominant 13ths, minor 9ths
- **Chromatic passing chords:** Half-step approaches to target chords (Db7 → C major)
- **Tritone substitution (embryonic):** Especially in Art Tatum
- **Secondary dominant chains:** II–V–I through multiple tonicizations
- **♭VI borrowed chords** from parallel minor

---

### Art Tatum: Beyond Stride

Art Tatum (1909–1956) represents a qualitative leap:

- **Reharmonization:** Introduces unexpected chord substitutions mid-phrase — a sudden tritone
  substitute, a brief excursion through a distant key center, then a snap back
- **Classical technique:** Incorporates Liszt/Chopin-style arpeggiated chord runs and tremolo basses
- **Walking tenths:** Left hand walks in parallel 10th intervals — root + 3rd an octave up — moving
  through a progression chromatically or diatonically. Both notes of the 10th voice-led together.
  Right hand simultaneously plays melody and improvised counterpoint.
- **Rubato introductions:** Opens pieces with a highly reharmonized, freely paced introduction
  before launching into stride tempo

## 3. Blues Piano (1900s–present)

**Key figures:** Bessie Smith (with James P. Johnson), Memphis Slim, Pete Johnson, various regional
styles

### The 12-Bar Blues Form

Standard 12-bar blues in C — all three chords are **dominant 7ths**:

```
Bar  1: I7  (C7)
Bar  2: I7  (C7)
Bar  3: I7  (C7)
Bar  4: I7  (C7)
Bar  5: IV7 (F7)
Bar  6: IV7 (F7)
Bar  7: I7  (C7)
Bar  8: I7  (C7)
Bar  9: V7  (G7)
Bar 10: IV7 (F7)  — or V7 in "quick change" variant
Bar 11: I7  (C7)
Bar 12: V7  (G7)  — the turnaround bar
```

**Critical:** The I chord is a dominant 7th, not a major chord. This creates a pervasive unresolved
quality that is the blues sound — "wrong" by classical standards but foundational to the style.

**Quick change variant:** Bar 2 moves to IV7 before returning to I7 in bars 3–4. Common in Chicago
and electric blues.

---

### Blue Notes

| Blue note | In key of C | Character                                                     |
| --------- | ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ♭3        | E♭          | Most characteristic — minor third over a major/dominant chord |
| ♭5 (= ♯4) | G♭ (= F#)   | Tritone above root; used as passing note for tension          |
| ♭7        | B♭          | Present in the I7 chord itself; both "blue" and a chord tone  |

**On piano:** Blue notes are approximated by:

- **Grace note from half step below** (mimicking a vocal scoop or guitar bend)
- **Simultaneous major/minor 3rd cluster:** Playing E♭ and E♮ together, then releasing the E♭ —
  simulates a pitch bend "resolving"
- **Glissando** between adjacent black keys

---

### Left Hand Patterns

**1. Steady bass (octaves or 5ths):**

- Simple quarter-note bass in octaves or open 5ths on the root
- Drone-like foundation; common in Delta and rural blues piano **2. Shuffle bass:**
- Alternating pattern between root (or root-octave) and 5th (or 6th) of the chord
- In **shuffle rhythm** (swung 8ths): long-short, approximated as dotted-eighth/sixteenth but
  performed as triplet-eighth/triplet-quarter
- Pattern: C (beat 1) / G (swung "and") / C (beat 2) / A (swung "and") — root–5th / root–6th
  alternation **3. Riff-based bass:** Short repeating left-hand figures that define the character of
  the piece.

---

### Right Hand

- **Single-note pentatonic lines:** Minor pentatonic (1–♭3–4–5–♭7) and blues scale (adds ♭5)
- **Chord-melody stabs:** Full chord-stabs and melody simultaneously (Texas style)
- **Riffs:** Short repeating melodic figures, 2–4 beats; the melodic equivalent of the LH ostinato
- **Call-and-response:** RH melody phrase, then space; LH fill or RH chord; then next phrase

---

### Turnarounds (last 2 bars or bar 12)

| Pattern           | Example in C                            |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| I–VI–II–V         | C7 – A7 – Dm7 – G7                      |
| I–♯I°–II–V        | C – C♯dim7 – Dm7 – G7                   |
| Chromatic descent | C7 – B7 – B♭7 – A7 (four quarter notes) |
| Gospel-flavored   | I – ♭VII – ♭VI – V                      |

## 4. Boogie-Woogie (1920s–1940s)

**Key figures:** Pine Top Smith, Meade Lux Lewis, Albert Ammons, Pete Johnson

### Core Concept: Eight-to-the-Bar

Boogie-woogie is built on a **continuous eighth-note left-hand ostinato** — **eight eighth notes per
bar** in 4/4. The left hand never stops. The eighth notes are played with a slight **swing feel**
(long-short triplet subdivision, not perfectly even).

---

### Left Hand: Common Boogie Bass Patterns

**1. The walking pattern (most common):** In C (repeating each bar over the C7 chord):

```
C – E – G – A – B♭ – A – G – E  (8 eighth notes)
```

Outlines root, 3rd, 5th, 6th, and ♭7 — chord tones + blues scale.

**2. Double bass / parallel octaves:** Same walking pattern in parallel octaves in the left hand.
Creates enormous physical volume. Albert Ammons specialty.

**3. Root–5th hammer pattern:** C / G / C / G / C / G / C / G — alternating root and 5th in eighth
notes. Stripped-down; used as a foundation.

**4. The chord roll:** Broken chords or arpeggios rather than single-line walking — a rolling,
churning texture.

**5. Honky Tonk Train Blues pattern (Meade Lux Lewis):** A specific figure mimicking a train rhythm
— mechanically repetitive with slight rhythmic displacement suggesting train wheels on track joints.

---

### How to Notate Boogie Bass

Three approaches in standard notation:

| Method                        | Description                                                                       | Best for                                                       |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Straight 8ths + swing marking | Write all 8ths as equal values; add "swing" indication at top                     | Most common in published editions                              |
| Explicit triplet notation     | Write dotted-quarter/eighth within a triplet group                                | Most precise but clutters the score                            |
| 12/8 time signature           | Each beat has 3 eighth notes; boogie pattern falls naturally on the 8th-note grid | Most unambiguous for AI composition; eliminates swing markings |

**For AI composition (ABC or `**kern`):** 12/8 is recommended as the most unambiguous representation.

---

### Right Hand

- **Riffs:** Short repeating melodic figures (2–4 beats) that lock into the groove
- **Cross-rhythms:** Right-hand figures that repeat every 3 eighth notes against the left hand's
  8-to-the-bar — 3-against-8 polyrhythm
- **Chord stabs:** Percussive chord hits on offbeat positions
- **Blues/pentatonic runs:** Single-note passages from the blues scale

---

### Relationship to Blues Form

Boogie-woogie is almost entirely 12-bar blues. The left-hand pattern transposes to each chord:

- Bars 1–4: C boogie pattern (C walking bass)
- Bars 5–6: F boogie pattern (same shape starting on F)
- Bars 7–8: Return to C boogie
- Bar 9: G boogie pattern
- Bar 10: F boogie pattern
- Bars 11–12: C with turnaround

## Cross-Style Evolution

```
Ragtime (1895–1920)
  ↓ Expands register, speeds tempo, enriches harmony, adds improvisation
Stride (1920s–1940s)
  ↓ + Blues harmonic language + motoric energy
Early Jazz Piano (1930s–40s: Hines, Wilson, Nat King Cole)

Blues (1900s–present)
  ↓ Specific left-hand solution: ostinato replaces static bass
Boogie-Woogie (1920s–1940s)
```

**Shared harmonic core across all four styles:** Dominant 7th chords on I, IV, and V; secondary
dominant chains; chromatic approach chords. These styles are mutually intelligible and allow for
natural genre blending.
